One Factory, Five Storefronts: The Duplicate-Listing Problem Your Shortlist Doesn't Account For

Supplier Intel

When you pull candidate suppliers from a B2B marketplace — or hand a sourcing brief to an AI tool trained on one — you're implicitly assuming each listing represents a distinct company. That assumption breaks more often than most shortlists account for.

It's an open pattern in B2B sourcing that a single factory, or a single trading company, can run several storefronts under different registered names on the same marketplace, sometimes across more than one marketplace at once. Buyers describe near-identical factory photos turning up under different company names, and wildly inconsistent quotes for what turns out to be the same production line. None of this is necessarily fraud in the legal sense — multi-storefront selling is a known, even platform-acknowledged, pattern. But it quietly breaks something a buyer relies on without realizing it: the assumption that five quotes represent five independent options.

Why it matters for a shortlist

A list of ten "different" suppliers that's actually three underlying companies isn't giving you price competition or capacity redundancy — it's giving you the illusion of both. If a sourcing brief calls for split-sourcing across unrelated suppliers for risk reasons, duplicate storefronts can quietly defeat that requirement without a single listing ever reading as a red flag on its own.

What an evidence-based check looks like

Domain registration records, physical address matches, phone-number reuse across listings, and overlapping factory photography are all checkable signals that two "different" storefronts might trace back to the same underlying entity — the same kind of cross-referencing that catches a distributor using manufacturer language, just applied across listings instead of within one. No single signal proves common ownership on its own; taken together, they're evidence worth having before treating a shortlist as more diverse than it actually is.

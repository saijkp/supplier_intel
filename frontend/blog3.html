Why a Chinese Supplier's "Manufacturer" Claim Means Nothing Until You Check the Business License Number

Supplier Intel

Ask a sourcing consultant how they check whether a Chinese supplier is actually the manufacturer it claims to be, and most will mention something: the marketplace badge, the factory photos, the way the sales rep talks about MOQs. Fewer will mention the one document that's actually tied to a single registered legal entity — the business license, and the 18-character Unified Social Credit Code (USCC) printed on it.

Check the number, not the name

An English trading name on a storefront, a cold email, or even a company's own website is a translation choice, not a legal identity — and it frequently doesn't match the registered Chinese legal entity name character-for-character. The USCC is what actually ties a specific claim back to one registered legal person. China's State Administration for Market Regulation publishes the authoritative public registry against that code, independent of whatever the supplier's own PDF scan of their license says.

What a registry match tells you — and what it doesn't

Matching the code against the registry surfaces real signal: registered legal name, registration status (active, under investigation, revoked, in dissolution), registered business scope, and registration date. A business scope that includes manufacturing is a meaningfully different finding from one that reads wholesale or trading — and it's evidence you can check independently of anything the supplier tells you directly.

What it doesn't tell you is whether that registered entity actually owns the machinery, runs the production line, or makes the product itself rather than quietly subcontracting to someone else. A clean, active registration with the right scope rules out the cheapest version of misrepresentation — a shell entity or a mismatched name hiding behind a scanned license graphic — without settling the harder, separate question of real operational capability.

Evidence, not a verdict

That's the same discipline we apply to every registry-backed check we run, UK Companies House included: a registry lookup is evidence to weigh against everything else you're gathering, not a verdict that replaces it. A missing or mismatched code isn't proof of fraud on its own — it's a specific, concrete thing worth asking the supplier to resolve directly, the same way a reused factory photo or an unregistered certification number is.

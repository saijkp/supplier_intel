The ISO 9001 badge on a supplier's site isn't proof — here's the three-minute check that is

Supplier Intel

Most sourcing checklists tell you to "confirm ISO 9001." Very few tell you what confirming actually means. In practice, a lot of verification stops at "the certificate PDF exists and has the right logo on it" — which is close to no check at all. A certificate is trivially easy to photograph, reuse, or let lapse quietly while the badge stays on the homepage.

A certificate is only meaningful if you can independently trace it back to the body that issued it. That means checking three things, not one:

Is the issuing body itself accredited?

ISO 9001 certificates only carry weight when issued by a certification body that is itself accredited by a recognised national accreditation body, in turn a member of the IAF (International Accreditation Forum). A certificate from an unaccredited or unfamiliar issuer isn't necessarily fake, but it isn't independently checkable either — treat it as unverified rather than assume either way.

Does the certificate's scope actually cover what you're buying?

An ISO 9001 certificate is scoped to specific processes and product lines, not a blanket claim about the whole company. A supplier can hold a perfectly genuine certificate that simply doesn't cover the product category you're sourcing. The scope line on the certificate, not the badge on the website, is the thing to read.

Is it current?

Certificates run on a cycle with periodic surveillance audits; a certificate that's lapsed or approaching expiry without a listed renewal is a different situation from one recently reissued.

None of this requires a paid tool. Certification bodies and accreditation registries publish searchable databases specifically so a buyer can look up a certificate by number or company name without going through the supplier at all — that's the whole point of an independent registry versus a claim printed on a page.

If a supplier's certificate doesn't show up in the registry the issuing body says it should, or the scope doesn't match, that's evidence worth having before you rely on the claim — not proof of anything on its own, just a gap worth asking the supplier to close.

The same discipline, applied consistently

This is the same approach we apply to every claim-type we check: a certification claim gets checked against the actual issuing registry, the same way a factory photo gets checked against where else it appears online, or a "manufacturer" claim gets checked against registration records. Evidence-gathered, human-reviewed — never "verified supplier."
